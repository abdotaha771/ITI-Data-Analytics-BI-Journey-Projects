PART D – DOES COMMUTING ACTUALLY HURT PERFORMANCE?
Summary of Requirements, Implementation, and Results

1. D1 – LOOK BEFORE YOU CALCULATE
Requirements:
- Draw a scatter plot with commute_minutes on the X-axis and final_score on the Y-axis.
- Describe the direction and strength of the relationship.
- Calculate the correlation between commute_minutes and final_score.
- Calculate correlations between study_hours_weekly and final_score, and sleep_hours and final_score.
- Rank the relationships by strength.

Implementation:
- A scatter plot was created in Power BI for commute_minutes vs final_score.
- Correlation measures were created for commute time, study hours, and sleep hours.

Results:
- Commute time vs final score: r = -0.11 → weak negative correlation.
- Study hours per week vs final score: r = 0.35 → strongest relationship, positive.
- Sleep hours vs final score: r = 0.02 → almost no linear relationship.

Ranking by strength:
1. Study hours vs final score: 0.35 (strongest of the three)
2. Commute time vs final score: -0.11 (weak negative)
3. Sleep hours vs final score: 0.02 (almost no linear relationship)

Interpretation:
Study hours are more strongly associated with final score than commute time. The commute relationship is weak, so it should not be overstated.


2. D2 – THE CAUSATION QUESTION
Question:
Does the negative correlation prove that long commutes cause lower scores?

Answer:
No. Correlation does not prove causation. The result only shows that students with longer commute times tend to have slightly lower final scores.

Alternative explanations:
- Distance / socioeconomic factors: students living farther away may have different access to educational resources or study support.
- Time available for studying: students with long commutes may have less time for studying and homework. In this case, study time could be more important than commuting itself.

Evidence needed to establish causation:
A randomized controlled study would provide stronger evidence. Students could be randomly assigned to locations/branches with different commute times, while collecting information such as study hours, previous academic performance, socioeconomic background, distance from home, attendance, and access to educational resources.

Result/interpretation:
The report is designed to compare the difference between group means with the standard deviation within the groups. A small mean gap compared with the within-group standard deviation indicates a limited practical difference.

Important statistical conclusion:
This analysis uses a sample rather than the entire ITI population. If the survey were repeated with different students, the exact gap would likely change. This variation is related to sampling variability / sampling error.

OVERALL CONCLUSION
The analysis does NOT provide evidence that commuting itself causes lower performance. Commute time has only a weak negative correlation with final score (r = -0.11). Study hours have a noticeably stronger positive relationship with final score (r = 0.35), while sleep hours have almost no linear relationship (r = 0.02).

Therefore, the data suggests that study time may be a more relevant factor to investigate than commute time. However, these are observational relationships and cannot by themselves establish causation.
