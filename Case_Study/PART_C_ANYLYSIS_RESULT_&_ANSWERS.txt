Main question: is the commute problem real, and how big is it?
======================================================================

The commute problem is real, but the claim that most students travel about an hour is not true for the typical student. The median commute is 33.05 minutes, while the mean is 45.30 minutes. This difference matters because the mean is being pulled upward by a smaller group of students with much longer trips, especially from Cairo.



Summary:
The overall evidence shows that the commute issue exists, but it is not a problem for most students in the same way the headline claim suggests. Most students commute around 33 minutes, while a smaller group of students have much longer journeys, which is why the average rises to 45.30 minutes.

C3. Spread, and why the average alone is a lie

Report the standard deviation and the IQR for commute time. They tell different stories. Why?

Verified spread values:
- Standard deviation: 39.58 minutes
- IQR: 37.98 minutes
- Coefficient of variation: 87.37%

The standard deviation is large because it measures how far values spread around the mean, and it is heavily affected by extreme long commutes. The IQR is more stable because it focuses on the middle 50% of the data and ignores the most extreme values.

Using the 1.5 x IQR rule:
- Lower fence: -36.59 minutes
- Upper fence: 115.31 minutes

This means 15 students are outside the normal commuting range and have unusually long commutes. These long trips distort the mean and are exactly why averages alone can be misleading. Importantly, 12 of these 15 outliers are from Cairo alone, which shows that the extreme commuting problem is concentrated in that branch.

Useful policy thresholds:
- More than 60 minutes: 60 of the 254 students with recorded commute times (23.6%).
- More than 90 minutes: 23 of the 254 students with recorded commute times (9.1%).

The 90-minute group is a smaller, clearly defined group facing the greatest commute burden and is suitable for a low-cost pilot. The 60-minute group can be considered later if the pilot shows a measurable benefit.

Conclusion:
A small group of students with very long journeys pulls the mean upward. That is why the average alone is not enough to describe the commute problem. The spread shows that the issue is real, but it is concentrated in a subset of students rather than affecting most of the class. Cairo is the main driver of the problem: 12 out of the 15 outliers come from Cairo.
